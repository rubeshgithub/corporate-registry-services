You've incorporated your Canadian corporation, and you probably received a certificate of incorporation and a copy of your articles. But did you also receive **by-laws**? A surprising number of Canadian corporations discover — often years later, during a financing or sale — that they were never given by-laws, or that the by-laws they have are missing crucial provisions.

This guide covers what corporate by-laws are under Canadian law, the difference between articles and by-laws, what every set of by-laws must include, how by-laws are enacted and amended, and what to do if you incorporated without them.

## What are corporate by-laws?

Corporate by-laws are the internal operating rules of a corporation — the constitution of how the corporation runs itself day to day. Where the articles of incorporation define *what* the corporation is (its name, share structure, directors, jurisdiction), the by-laws define *how* the corporation operates: how directors are appointed and meet, how officers exercise their authority, how shares can be transferred, how meetings are held, how notices are given.

Under the *Canada Business Corporations Act* (s. 103) and every provincial equivalent — Ontario's *OBCA*, Alberta's *ABCA*, BC's *Business Corporations Act*, Quebec's *QBCA* — the directors *may* make by-laws for the corporation. In practice, "may" is treated as "must" for any serious commercial purpose. Every bank, every buyer's lawyer, and every court will assume your corporation has by-laws — and will be looking for a specific by-law to answer specific questions.

## Articles vs. by-laws: what's the difference?

The two documents are often confused because both are foundational to the corporation, but they serve different purposes:

**Articles of incorporation** — the corporation's founding document filed with the government at incorporation. Articles are **public** — anyone can obtain them from the corporate registry. They define:

- The corporate name (or numbered designation for a numbered corporation)
- The registered office jurisdiction
- The share structure — class(es), authorized number, rights and restrictions per class
- Restrictions on share transfer (for a private corporation)
- Number of directors (or a range)
- Restrictions on the corporation's business, if any

**By-laws** — internal operating rules made by the directors. By-laws are **private** — they stay in the minute book and are not filed with the government. They define:

- How directors are appointed, removed, and meet
- Quorum requirements for board and shareholder meetings
- Officer positions and their authorities
- How share transfers are approved and recorded
- Notice periods for meetings
- The corporation's fiscal year
- Signing authority for cheques, contracts, and other binding documents
- Indemnification of directors and officers
- The corporate seal (whether required)

You can amend either document, but the mechanics differ: articles amendments require a special shareholder resolution AND a filing with the corporate registry, while by-law amendments require only a directors' resolution confirmed by an ordinary shareholder resolution at the next annual meeting.

## What must by-laws include?

Canadian corporate law doesn't prescribe a specific list of by-law provisions — the *CBCA* and provincial acts leave the substance up to the directors. In practice, a standard set of by-laws for a small Canadian corporation covers:

**Meetings of directors:**
- Quorum requirements (typically majority)
- Calling meetings and notice periods
- Voting procedures and tie-breaking
- Resolutions in writing in lieu of meetings

**Officers:**
- List of officer positions (President, Secretary, Treasurer, etc.)
- Appointment and removal procedures
- Duties and authorities of each position
- Whether officers can hold multiple positions

**Shareholders' meetings:**
- Annual meeting requirement
- Notice period
- Quorum requirements
- Voting procedures
- Chairperson selection
- Written resolutions in lieu of meetings

**Share issuance and transfer:**
- Restrictions on transfer of shares (for private corporations)
- Procedures for issuing and cancelling share certificates
- Right of first refusal, if applicable

**Corporate governance:**
- Fiscal year
- Corporate seal (if used)
- Banking arrangements
- Signing authorities for contracts and cheques
- Indemnification of directors and officers

**Shareholder consents:**
- Procedures for unanimous written resolutions
- Notice waivers

## Standard vs. custom by-laws

Most Canadian corporations use standard general by-laws — often called "By-Law No. 1" — covering the topics above with well-tested boilerplate provisions. These are appropriate for the vast majority of small and mid-sized corporations because:

- They cover every scenario that arises in normal corporate operation
- They match the language used by banks, lawyers, and auditors
- They can be amended easily if the corporation grows or its structure changes

**Custom by-laws** become worthwhile in specific situations:

- **Shareholder agreements** that impose non-standard voting or transfer restrictions
- **Multi-class share structures** with complex conversion or preference rights
- **Family holding corporations** with succession or estate planning requirements
- **Joint venture corporations** with negotiated governance provisions between the founders

For most corporations, By-Law No. 1 (general by-laws) is enough. Custom provisions can be added later if the corporation's structure evolves.

## How to enact and amend by-laws in Canada

Under the *CBCA* s. 103 and provincial equivalents, the mechanics of enacting and amending by-laws follow a two-step process:

1. **Directors' resolution** — the directors pass a resolution enacting the by-law (or amending an existing by-law). The by-law takes effect immediately from the date of the directors' resolution.
2. **Shareholder confirmation** — at the next shareholders' meeting (usually the annual meeting), the shareholders confirm the by-law by ordinary resolution. If shareholders confirm, the by-law continues in effect. If shareholders decline to confirm, the by-law ceases to have effect from that point forward — though any actions taken under the by-law before the meeting remain valid.

In practice, the two-step process is completed at the same time for a small corporation with unanimous shareholder-director overlap: the directors pass the resolution, and the same people sign a unanimous shareholder written resolution confirming the by-law. Both resolutions go in the minute book together with the by-law itself.

## What if you incorporated without by-laws?

This is one of the most common gaps in Canadian corporate records. Many online incorporation services deliver only the articles of incorporation and a certificate — leaving the corporation without by-laws, organizational resolutions, or a proper minute book. The corporation exists legally but is running without any documented governance rules.

The problem surfaces during due diligence: a buyer's lawyer or lender's lawyer asks for the by-laws, finds nothing, and either demands they be reconstructed retroactively or holds back on the transaction until they are. Reconstructing by-laws retroactively is straightforward — a professionally drafted By-Law No. 1 dated at the current date, enacted by directors' resolution and confirmed by shareholders' resolution, brings the corporation into compliance.
