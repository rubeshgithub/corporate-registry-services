Yes — a non-resident can open a business bank account in Canada. No law prevents it. But the account belongs to a **Canadian-registered company**, not to you personally, and every bank applies its own verification rules under Canada's anti-money-laundering regime — which, at most major banks, still means at least one signing officer showing up at a branch in person. The whole exercise comes down to two things: having the corporation set up correctly before you approach a bank, and walking in with the exact document pack the bank will ask for.

This guide covers both readers: the founder still deciding whether to incorporate in Canada, and the owner whose Canadian corporation already exists and now needs the account. If that second one is you, start by checking your corporation's status in the search box above — a company that isn't active and in good standing gets turned away before the documents even come out.

## Why do you need a Canadian company before the bank will talk to you?

Canadian banks open business accounts for Canadian entities. As a non-resident you have two routes in: **incorporate a Canadian corporation** (the common route — a new company, owned by you, registered in a Canadian jurisdiction) or **register your existing foreign company extra-provincially** as a corporation carrying on business in a province. Either way, the bank's first demand is proof the entity exists on a Canadian registry.

Where you incorporate matters more for non-residents than for anyone else, because director residency rules differ:

- **Alberta and British Columbia have no director residency requirement** — a corporation owned and directed entirely by non-residents is fine. This is why both provinces are the default choice for non-resident founders; see our guides to [incorporating in Alberta](/articles/how-to-incorporate-in-alberta) and [B.C. incorporation costs](/articles/cost-to-incorporate-in-bc).
- **Federal (CBCA) incorporation requires at least 25% of directors to be resident Canadians** (at least one, where there are fewer than four directors) — a hard stop for most non-resident-owned startups. The trade-offs are covered in [federal vs. provincial incorporation](/articles/federal-vs-provincial-incorporation-canada).

Two more things every non-resident corporation needs on day one, because both the registry and the bank will ask:

- **A registered office in Canada.** A real street address in the province of incorporation, where legal documents are served. If you have no Canadian address, CRS provides a [registered office service](/incorporation) at {{price:registered-office}}/year.
- **A minute book.** The corporation's legal record — articles, by-laws, registers of directors, shareholders and beneficial owners, and the resolutions that authorize things like bank accounts. Banks increasingly ask to see it. CRS builds a complete [digital minute book](/minute-books/digital-minute-book-canada) from {{price:minute-book-young-crs}}.

## What documents do banks ask for?

The exact list varies by bank, but published requirements converge on the same pack. For a Canadian corporation owned by non-residents, expect to produce:

| Document | What it proves | Where it comes from |
|---|---|---|
| Certificate of incorporation + articles | The company exists | Issued at incorporation; copies obtainable from the registry |
| **Certificate of status / good standing** | The company is active and compliant | Ordered from the registry — [{{price:good-standing}} + GST via CRS](/good-standing) |
| **Corporate profile report** | Current directors, registered office, as the registry sees them | [{{price:profile-report}} + GST via CRS](/articles/corporate-profile-report-canada) — banks use it to verify who can sign |
| **Board resolution authorizing the account** | The signers are authorized by the corporation | Drafted and passed by the directors — [CRS prepares it for {{price:director-resolution}} + GST](/articles/director-resolutions-in-canada) |
| CRA Business Number | The company is registered for tax | See the BN section below |
| ID + proof of address for each signer | Who the humans are | Passport + utility bill/bank statement, per the bank's list |
| Beneficial-ownership information | Who owns 25%+ of the company | Your corporation's registers — see [transparency registers](/articles/corporate-transparency-registers-canada) |

That last row trips people up: under the federal anti-money-laundering rules (the PCMLTFA), banks must identify and verify every individual who owns or controls **25% or more** of the corporation — not just the directors. A properly maintained [minute book](/guides/what-is-a-corporate-minute-book-canada) with up-to-date registers is the clean answer; a shoebox of incorporation papers is not.

**Foreign documents:** since **January 11, 2024, Canada is a member of the Apostille Convention** ([gov.bc.ca](https://www2.gov.bc.ca/gov/content/governments/government-id/guide-to-the-authentication-of-documents/about/apostille)), so documents from fellow member countries need an apostille from the issuing country rather than the old embassy-legalization chain; non-member countries still go the authentication-plus-legalization route. Expect certified translation for anything not in English or French.

## Do you have to visit a branch in person?

Usually, yes — this is the wall most non-resident owners hit. Published guides consistently report that major Canadian banks require at least one director or signing officer to verify identity **in person at a branch**, and remote onboarding for a non-resident-owned corporation is the exception, not the rule. Every bank sets its own policy, so confirm before you fly — but plan around three realistic paths:

- **Make the visit count.** If you're coming to Canada anyway, book the account appointment and arrive with the full document pack above. Incomplete paperwork is the most common reason the account takes weeks instead of days.
- **Use a bank with a presence in your home country.** Several Canadian banks have US and international affiliates, and some can start verification where you live. Ask the bank directly — policies differ by institution and by country.
- **Bridge with a money-service platform.** Fintech platforms (Wise Business, Payoneer and similar) onboard remotely and give you CAD account details for invoicing while you arrange the traditional account. Understand what they are: payment institutions, **not CDIC-insured banks**, and some Canadian counterparties, lenders and the CRA may still expect a real bank account eventually.

Some owners also appoint a **Canadian-resident signing officer** they trust so a local person can attend the branch — a business decision with real control implications, so take advice before handing anyone signing authority.

## How do you get a CRA Business Number as a non-resident?

The Business Number (BN) is the CRA's 9-digit identifier for your company, and banks routinely ask for it. For most new corporations it's generated automatically when the incorporation is registered — typically no separate application is needed for the BN itself. Program accounts (GST/HST, payroll, import/export) are a separate registration, and the CRA runs a dedicated route for non-residents, since its standard online registration assumes a Canadian SIN — see the CRA's [business-number registration page](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/businesses/topics/business-registration/business-number-program-account/how-register.html). Tax registrations are your accountant's territory; CRS handles the registry side — the corporation, its filings and its records — not CRA filings.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can a foreigner open a business bank account in Canada?

Yes. There is no citizenship or residency requirement in law — but the account is opened for a Canadian-registered company, and each bank applies its own identity-verification policy under federal anti-money-laundering rules. In practice that means a Canadian corporation (or extra-provincial registration), a complete document pack, and usually at least one signing officer verifying identity in person at a branch.

### Can I open the account before incorporating in Canada?

Not a Canadian business account in the company's name — the entity has to exist on a Canadian registry first, because the certificate and articles of incorporation are the first documents every bank requests. Incorporation in Alberta or B.C., where no director needs to be a Canadian resident, typically takes a day or two, so the corporation is rarely the slow step.

### Do all Canadian banks require an in-person visit?

Policies vary by bank and change over time, but published guides consistently report that major banks require at least one director or signing officer to verify identity in person at a branch. Some banks with international affiliates can begin verification in your home country, and fintech platforms onboard fully remotely — though those are payment institutions, not CDIC-insured banks. Confirm the current policy with your chosen bank before travelling.

### What documents does a non-resident-owned corporation need for the bank?

Certificate and articles of incorporation, a certificate of status proving the corporation is active, a corporate profile report showing current directors, a board resolution authorizing the account and its signers, the CRA Business Number, passports and proof of address for every signer, and beneficial-ownership details for anyone holding 25% or more. Foreign documents from Apostille Convention countries need an apostille; others need authentication and legalization, plus certified translation if not in English or French.

### What if my corporation isn't in good standing?

Fix it before approaching a bank — a corporation showing inactive, struck or behind on annual returns will be refused. Usually the cure is filing the outstanding annual return with the registry, which restores good standing quickly; a corporation already dissolved for non-filing needs a revival first.
