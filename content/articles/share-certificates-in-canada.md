## What is a share certificate?

A share certificate is a formal document issued by a corporation confirming that a named person or entity owns a specific number and class of shares. It functions as physical (or digital) evidence of ownership — the same way a real-estate title confirms property ownership. The corporation's minute book keeps a copy of every certificate ever issued, numbered sequentially, together with the subscription agreement and directors' resolution that authorized it.

Under the *Canada Business Corporations Act* (s. 49) and every provincial equivalent — Ontario's *OBCA*, Alberta's *ABCA*, BC's *Business Corporations Act*, Quebec's *QBCA* — the corporation is required to maintain records of who holds which shares. The physical certificate itself is customary rather than strictly mandated, but running a corporation without certificates raises immediate red flags in any financing, sale, or CRA review.

## When do you need a share certificate?

Practically every time shares change hands, someone will ask for a certificate:

- **At incorporation** — the first shareholders subscribing for founder shares receive their certificates as part of the organizational minute book.
- **New investment or financing** — banks and lenders want to see the share register and the certificates behind it before approving a loan against corporate assets.
- **Adding a shareholder** — bringing a co-founder, family member, or employee onto the cap table triggers a full paper trail (see below).
- **Selling the corporation** — a share purchase agreement is impossible to close without producing certificates for the transferring shares.
- **Estate planning and wills** — a will that gifts shares to beneficiaries relies on certificates as evidence of the specific shares gifted.
- **CRA audit or dispute** — the tax authority will ask for share ownership records when reviewing shareholder loans, dividends, or capital gains.

## The share issuance paper trail

Issuing shares in Canada generates more minute book paperwork than any other routine event. Here is the complete four-step sequence, in order:

1. **Share subscription agreement.** The incoming shareholder submits a written offer to purchase shares, stating the class, number, and price. This agreement is signed and stored in the minute book with the corporation's records for that specific issuance.
2. **Directors' resolution approving the allotment.** The board formally accepts the subscription, confirms payment has been received, and directs the corporation to issue the shares. This is a written resolution signed by all directors when the corporation doesn't hold a formal meeting.
3. **Share certificate issued to the shareholder.** The certificate is prepared, numbered sequentially (Certificate No. 1, 2, 3…), signed by a director or officer, and delivered to the shareholder. A copy plus the certificate stub stays in the minute book.
4. **Register updates.** The register of shareholders, the securities register (the running record of every issuance and transfer by certificate number), and — where control of the corporation shifts — the register of individuals with significant control (ISC register) all need to be updated at the same time.

If any link in this chain is missing, the share issuance is legally questionable. Buyer's-side lawyers routinely find gaps in the subscription-resolution-certificate-register sequence during due diligence, and those gaps become renegotiation levers on the sale price or hold-back on the closing funds.

## What information must appear on a share certificate?

Canadian corporate law and typical bank/lawyer expectations require the following on every share certificate:

- **Corporation's full legal name** — matching the certificate of incorporation exactly.
- **Jurisdiction of incorporation** — federal, or the specific province.
- **Certificate number** — sequential, matching the securities register entry.
- **Shareholder's name** — full legal name of the individual or the exact legal name of the corporate entity.
- **Class of shares** — Common, Preferred, Class A, Class B, or whatever class structure the articles authorize.
- **Number of shares represented** — written and typically also spelled out in words.
- **Date of issuance.**
- **Signatures** — at least one director or officer, sometimes two, depending on the by-laws.
- **Corporate seal** — legally optional in most Canadian jurisdictions but often still expected on formal certificates.
- **Restrictions on transfer** — for private corporations, a legend describing any restrictions on transfer of the shares (e.g., "The transfer of the shares represented by this certificate is restricted by the articles of the corporation").

## Are digital share certificates legal in Canada?

Yes. Federal and provincial corporate statutes explicitly permit share certificates to be issued and stored electronically, provided they contain the same information as a paper certificate and can be produced in readable form for inspection at the registered office.

## Common mistakes with share certificates

The four most common errors in Canadian share certificates — all of which come up in due diligence:

- **Certificates issued without the underlying resolution.** The certificate exists but there is no directors' resolution in the minute book approving the allotment. Buyer's lawyer flags it, closing gets delayed.
- **Certificate numbers not sequential.** Skipping certificate numbers or reusing them makes the securities register impossible to reconcile.
- **Names don't match the register.** The certificate lists "John A. Smith" while the register lists "John Andrew Smith"; the mismatch is a technical failure that may need to be corrected retroactively.
- **Transfer restrictions missing.** Private corporations that omit the transfer-restriction legend can inadvertently lose their "private" status for regulatory purposes.
