You've signed a directors' resolution. You've issued shares and prepared a share subscription. Now what? A surprising number of Canadian business owners search for where to *file* these documents — expecting a government portal — and find nothing. That's because the answer is simpler and stricter than most people expect: **almost none of these documents get filed with the government. They go in your corporate minute book, kept at your registered office.**

This guide walks through each core corporate document — director resolutions, shareholder resolutions, annual resolutions, share subscriptions, share certificates, registers, and by-laws — and explains exactly where each one belongs, who signs it, and what must be updated when it's added.

If you're not sure what a minute book is in the first place, start with our guide: [What Is a Corporate Minute Book — And Why Canadian Corporations Need One](/guides/what-is-a-corporate-minute-book-canada).

## The short answer: nothing gets "filed" — it gets recorded

Under the *Canada Business Corporations Act* (s. 20) and every provincial equivalent (Ontario's OBCA s. 140, Alberta's ABCA, BC's BCA, Quebec's QBCA), a corporation must prepare and maintain records containing its articles, by-laws, minutes and resolutions, and its registers — and keep them at its **registered office** (or another place in Canada designated by the directors). These records can be a physical binder or a properly maintained digital minute book.

The government never sees your resolutions. What the government sees is your **annual return** and change filings (directors, address, etc.). The resolutions that *authorized* those changes live only in your minute book — and that's exactly what a CRA auditor, a bank, or a buyer's lawyer will ask to see.

## Director resolutions: where they go

A directors' resolution records a decision of the board — either passed at a meeting (recorded in minutes) or, far more commonly for small corporations, signed by all directors as a **resolution in writing** in lieu of a meeting.

Common director resolutions include: approving the issuance or transfer of shares, appointing officers, approving financial statements, declaring dividends, authorizing bank accounts and signing officers, and approving major contracts or loans.

**Where it goes:** in the minutes and resolutions section of your minute book, in chronological order, signed and dated. If the resolution changes directors or officers, the register of directors or register of officers must be updated at the same time — and if directors changed, a notice must also be filed with your corporate registry.

## Shareholder resolutions: where they go

Shareholder resolutions record decisions reserved for the corporation's owners: electing directors, appointing (or waiving) the auditor, approving fundamental changes like amendments to the articles, and confirming by-laws. An **ordinary resolution** needs a simple majority; a **special resolution** needs two-thirds.

**Where it goes:** same place — the resolutions section of the minute book, chronological, signed by the required shareholders. For single-shareholder corporations, a one-page written resolution of the sole shareholder does the job, but it still must exist and still must be in the book.

## Annual resolutions: the yearly package everyone forgets

Every Canadian corporation — even a one-person corporation — must pass **annual resolutions** each year. The typical annual package is:

- Shareholders' resolution electing (or re-electing) the directors
- Shareholders' resolution waiving the appointment of an auditor (for most private corporations)
- Shareholders' resolution approving the financial statements presented by the directors
- Directors' resolution approving the financial statements and appointing officers

Law firms sometimes call this the "change the dates" package — but skipping it means your corporation has no legally elected directors and no valid auditor waiver, which surfaces at the worst possible time: during a financing, a sale, or a CRA review.

## Share issuance: subscription, resolution, certificate, register — in that order

Issuing shares generates more minute book paperwork than any other routine event, and it's the family of documents people search for most. Here is the complete paper trail, in order:

1. **Share subscription** — the incoming shareholder's written offer to purchase shares, stating the class, number, and price. Goes in the minute book with the corporation's records for that issuance.
2. **Directors' resolution approving the allotment** — the board accepts the subscription, confirms payment, and directs that shares be issued. Goes in the resolutions section.
3. **Share certificate** — issued to the shareholder, numbered sequentially, signed. The shareholder keeps the original; a copy (and the certificate stub) stays in the minute book.
4. **Register updates** — the register of shareholders, the **securities register** (the running record of every issuance and transfer by certificate number), and, where shares change who controls the corporation, the **register of individuals with significant control (ISC register)** must all be updated.

If any link in that chain is missing, the share issuance is legally questionable — which is precisely what due-diligence lawyers look for. ## Registers: the part of the minute book that must always be current

Resolutions record events; registers record **state**. Every Canadian minute book must contain:

- **Register of directors** — every director, with appointment and cessation dates
- **Register of officers** — same, for officers
- **Register of shareholders / share ledger** — who holds what, by class
- **Securities register** — every issuance and transfer, by certificate number
- **ISC register** (federal and most provinces) — individuals with significant control, updated at least annually and within 15 days of learning of a change

Quebec corporations should note the QBCA's equivalent requirements and the *Registraire des entreprises*' transparency rules — the registers still live in the minute book, in French or English.

## By-laws and incorporation documents

Your **certificate and articles of incorporation** open the minute book — they are its first tab. Your **by-laws** (the corporation's internal operating rules) follow, together with the directors' and shareholders' resolutions that enacted them. ## Digital minute books are fully legal

Nothing in the CBCA or provincial statutes requires a red leather binder. Records may be kept electronically as long as they can be produced in intelligible form and remain accessible for inspection at the registered office. A well-organized [digital minute book](/minute-books/digital-minute-book-canada) is easier to keep current, easier to share with your accountant or lender, and impossible to leave in a filing cabinet during an audit.
