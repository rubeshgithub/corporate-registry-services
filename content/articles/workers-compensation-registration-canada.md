Workers' compensation registration is mandatory across Canada, and in most provinces the duty starts with your very first hire: Ontario and Nova Scotia give you 10 days, Alberta and New Brunswick 15, Saskatchewan 30 and Quebec 60, while PEI expects you to register before operations begin. The thresholds vary too — New Brunswick and Nova Scotia only require coverage once you have three workers, and Manitoba ties the duty to a list of mandatory industries. This guide covers who must register with which board, the deadline in all 13 provinces and territories, how premiums are calculated, and where owners and directors fit. Every figure below comes from the board's own website, verified at the time of writing — these are each board's rules, not ours, and boards change them: confirm against the board's current pages before you rely on a number.

## What is workers' compensation — and who runs it?

Workers' compensation is Canada's no-fault workplace injury insurance: employers fund it entirely through premiums, injured workers receive wage-loss and health-care benefits without having to prove fault, and in exchange they give up the right to sue the employer. It is run by twelve independent boards covering thirteen jurisdictions — every province and territory has its own, with one commission (the WSCC) covering both the Northwest Territories and Nunavut. There is no federal board for ordinary businesses: you register where your workers work.

Registering means opening an employer account with your board, classifying your business into an industry group, and then reporting payroll and paying premiums on the board's cycle. It's a separate obligation from your [CRA accounts](/articles/how-to-set-up-a-cra-gst-account) and from your corporate registry filings — three different offices, three different calendars.

## Do you have to register for WCB?

In most jurisdictions, yes — from the first worker. Alberta, BC, Saskatchewan, Ontario, Quebec, PEI, Newfoundland and Labrador, Yukon and the territories all require registration once you employ anyone, and "worker" is read broadly: full-time, part-time, casual and contract workers count, and in several provinces so do family members and even unpaid help.

The main exceptions:

- **New Brunswick** requires registration only once you have **three or more workers** at any time during the year (fewer than three can apply for voluntary coverage).
- **Nova Scotia** requires it when you operate in a **mandatory industry** and have **three or more workers at the same time**.
- **Manitoba** ties mandatory registration to its list of **mandatory industries** rather than a worker count.
- Most boards also exclude specific industries (Saskatchewan, for example, excludes farming and ranching) — and businesses outside the mandatory net can usually opt in with voluntary coverage.

Two wrinkles catch incorporated owners. In BC, a shareholder actively working in their own company is generally treated as that company's employee, so the company applies even if the owner is the only one working. In Newfoundland and Labrador, every employer doing business in the province must register, and in an incorporated entity all workers are covered — including owner-directors, even when the owner is the only worker. If you're not sure your situation requires an account, ask the board before deciding it doesn't: misclassifying is the expensive direction.

## How long do you have after hiring your first worker?

The deadlines are short, and they're board-specific:

- **10 days:** Ontario (10 calendar days from hiring your first employee), Nova Scotia (10 days from meeting the coverage conditions), Yukon (10 calendar days from starting operations), NWT/Nunavut (10 days from starting operations).
- **15 days:** Alberta (15 days from first employing a worker), New Brunswick (15 days from the start of the business).
- **30 days:** Saskatchewan (30 days from beginning work or hiring workers).
- **60 days:** Quebec (60 days from your first worker's first day — and you can register up to 30 days before they start).
- **Before you start:** PEI expects registration prior to the start of business operations.
- **No published day-count:** BC recommends applying about four weeks before you start hiring; Manitoba and Newfoundland and Labrador publish no day-count, but late registration still carries penalties — register when you begin employing.

## How are WCB premiums calculated?

Everywhere in Canada the formula is the same shape: **your industry's rate × your assessable payroll**, with rates quoted per $100 of payroll and each worker's earnings counted only up to the annual maximum in the table above. Your rate depends on the industry classification the board assigns at registration — not on your corporation's profits — and after a few years most boards adjust it up or down based on your own claims record.

An illustration (not a quote): at a $2.00 rate per $100, a company with $300,000 in assessable payroll pays about $6,000 a year. Rates in 2026 range from well under $1 per $100 in low-risk office classifications to several dollars in construction and transport, and some boards add fixed charges — Saskatchewan, for example, bills a $100 minimum annual account fee. You report actual payroll annually (with an estimate up front), so under- or over-reporting payroll is the most common way premiums go wrong.

## Are owners, partners and directors covered automatically?

Generally, no. Sole proprietors, partners and, in most provinces, directors are not "workers," so a workplace injury to the owner is not covered unless you buy the board's optional product. Every board sells one, under its own name: **personal coverage** in Alberta, Saskatchewan, Manitoba, New Brunswick and PEI; **Personal Coverage** (formerly Personal Optional Protection) in BC; **optional insurance** at Ontario's WSIB; **protection personnelle** at the CNESST; **optional personal coverage** at WorkplaceNL; and **Personal Optional Coverage** at the WSCC.

The two standouts: in Newfoundland and Labrador, once an incorporated business registers, owner-directors working in the business are covered like any other worker; and in Yukon, a working director of a corporation is automatically a worker of that corporation. Everywhere else, decide deliberately — an uninsured owner injured on site has neither WCB benefits nor, in most cases, the right to sue.

## What is a clearance letter — and who will ask for one?

A clearance letter (some boards say clearance certificate, or just "a clearance") is the board's confirmation that a business is registered and its account is in good standing. General contractors, commercial clients and government buyers routinely require one before a contractor sets foot on site and again before releasing final payment — without it, the hiring firm can be held liable for your unpaid premiums. Every board issues them, most instantly online, and they are free.

It's the workers' compensation cousin of the registry's [certificate of good standing](/guides/what-is-a-certificate-of-good-standing-canada): different office, same question — is this company in good standing where it's supposed to be registered?

## What happens if you register late — or not at all?

Three things, in increasing order of pain. First, late-registration penalties: Alberta charges 10% of the premium plus interest, Saskatchewan 5% per year, Yukon a 10% administrative fee, Manitoba 5% per calendar year once you're more than six months late. Second, retroactive premiums — boards assess back to when you should have registered, not when you did. Third, and much the largest: an injured worker can claim whether or not you registered, and the board can then bill an unregistered employer for the claim's costs on top of the unpaid premiums — a single serious injury can run far past what years of premiums would have cost.
