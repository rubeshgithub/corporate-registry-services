You're about to buy a business, extend trade credit, take on a supplier, or invest in a partnership — and the counterparty is a Canadian corporation. Before you sign, you need to know: does this entity actually exist? Are the people signing authorised to bind it? Are there past names, prior amalgamations, or a dissolution notice that changes the deal?

This guide covers exactly what a corporate search should uncover, which documents provide which facts, and where the traps sit — the mistakes we see buyers, lenders, and counterparties make in every deal cycle.

## What a Corporate Profile Report reveals

A [Corporate Profile Report](/profile-reports) is the government-issued record of a Canadian corporation, pulled directly from the provincial or federal registry. For due diligence, it answers eight critical questions on one PDF:

1. **Current legal name** — including any operating names / trade names on file
2. **Current status** — active, in default, dissolved, struck, amalgamated
3. **Registered office address** — where legal notice can be served
4. **Communication / mailing address** — where the registry sends correspondence
5. **Directors** — full legal names and addresses of every sitting director
6. **Shareholders** — where the registry captures them (BC, some others)
7. **Agent for service** — the individual authorised to accept notice
8. **Last document filed** — most recent registry activity, dated

If any of these red-flags — status not "Active," no directors on file, address is out of date, last filing was three years ago — the deal stops until you have an explanation.

## What the Profile Report *doesn't* show — and how to fill the gap

The Profile Report is a current snapshot. It doesn't include:

- **Prior directors** who have resigned or been replaced
- **Historical addresses** (registered office moves)
- **Past name changes**
- **Share transfer history**
- **Old resolutions** and shareholder actions
- **Prior amalgamation partners** (only the current amalgamated entity shows)

For any transaction where history matters — share purchase, buyout, litigation, deep credit review — you need the corporation's [Minute Book](/minute-books). The Minute Book is the corporation's complete historical record: articles, by-laws, all resolutions since incorporation, share registers, director registers, and every filing that has ever been submitted. Where the Profile Report is a *snapshot*, the Minute Book is the *film*.

## The due-diligence document stack

For most Canadian transactions, this is the minimum evidence file:

| Document | Cost | What it proves |
|---|---|---|
| [Corporate Profile Report](/profile-reports) | $49 all-in | Current legal existence, status, directors, address |
| [Certificate of Good Standing / Status](/good-standing) | $79 all-in | Corporation is compliant with all filings |
| [Minute Book](/minute-books) (buyer's counsel review) | $299+ | Full history: resolutions, share transfers, past directors, articles amendments |
| PPSA / RPMRR lien search | Varies by province | Registered security interests against corporate assets |
| CRA arrears letter | Free (owner-requested) | No outstanding taxes owing |

For lower-risk transactions (small trade credit, single-invoice supply, low-stakes vendor onboarding), the Profile Report alone answers most questions. For higher-risk transactions (share purchase, financing, guaranteed line of credit), you need the full stack.

## The five most common due-diligence traps

**Trap 1: Signing with a corporation that's been struck.** Registries dissolve corporations for failing to file annual returns. A struck corporation can't hold assets or sign contracts under its former name. Every Profile Report shows current status — check first.

**Trap 2: Signing with an unauthorised director.** The person across the table introduces themselves as CEO or "director." The Profile Report lists actual sitting directors. If their name isn't on it, they may not have authority to bind the corporation. Get evidence of directors' resolution before you sign.

**Trap 3: Signing with a numbered company that recently amalgamated.** When two corporations amalgamate, the surviving entity may or may not carry the historical liabilities of both predecessors. The Profile Report notes amalgamation status; the [Minute Book](/minute-books) shows the amalgamation agreement itself.

**Trap 4: Trusting an old Profile Report.** A report pulled six months ago is out of date. Directors change, addresses change, corporations get struck. Order fresh at the point of contract signing, not before.

**Trap 5: Not verifying operating name vs. legal name.** Businesses often trade under names different from their legal corporate name. The Profile Report shows any operating names on file. If someone hands you a contract in the operating name only, the underlying corporation is what actually signed — verify both.

## When to order what

If you're doing due diligence at scale (vetting suppliers, running KYC, extending trade credit to many customers), a rolling Profile Report policy at contract renewal is the standard best practice. If you're buying a business, the sequence is:

1. **Letter of intent stage** — Profile Report + Certificate of Good Standing on both buyer and seller entities
2. **Post-LOI diligence** — Minute Book review (buyer's counsel), PPSA searches, CRA arrears
3. **Closing week** — Fresh Certificate of Good Standing dated within 14 days of close

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I do a corporate search for free on government websites?

Yes and no. Every provincial and federal registry offers a free basic search — you can look up a company name and see whether it exists. But the *certified* Profile Report (the document banks, buyers, and lawyers accept as evidence) is not free — it's a paid government-issued PDF. The free view shows a fraction of the information and is not signed or sealed.

### How current is a Corporate Profile Report?

The report reflects the registry's state as of the retrieval timestamp on the PDF. That timestamp is the date of authority. For high-stakes transactions, order the report within 14 days of the deal-closing event.

### What if the corporation doesn't appear in the search?

If a corporate name search returns no results, three possibilities: (1) the name you have isn't the legal name — try the business number; (2) the corporation is registered in a different jurisdiction; (3) the corporation was never actually incorporated. Ask the counterparty for their Business Number (9-digit CRA number) — that's the surest identifier.

### Do I need to search every province separately?

Not usually. A corporation is registered in one jurisdiction (its incorporating jurisdiction). Extra-provincial registrations show as *extra-provincial* filings — visible in that province's registry — but the primary record lives in the incorporating jurisdiction. If you don't know where a corporation is incorporated, start with a [Canada-wide search](/canada-corporations-search).
