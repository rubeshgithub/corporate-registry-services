You've decided to sue, or a demand letter isn't getting a response, or a small-claims filing is going in tomorrow. Before you file or serve, you need one document: the corporation's exact legal name, its registered office for service, and confirmation it still exists. Get any of these wrong and the claim can be struck for defect — or you spend six months chasing a corporation that dissolved before your first hearing.

This guide covers exactly what plaintiff's counsel needs from a corporate search, how to read the Profile Report for litigation purposes, and where the traps sit.

## What litigation demands that the Profile Report answers

Every civil filing or demand against a Canadian corporation requires four facts:

1. **The corporation's exact legal name** — as recorded on the corporate registry, including *"Inc."*, *"Ltd."*, or *"Corporation"* (missing these is a filing defect).
2. **Its registered office address** — where service is made under provincial Rules of Civil Procedure.
3. **Its current status** — you cannot sue a dissolved corporation.
4. **Its sitting directors** — needed if you're pleading director liability under the *Business Corporations Act*, environmental statutes, or CRA-source-deductions provisions.

A [Corporate Profile Report](/profile-reports) gives you all four on one PDF, sealed by the registry, dated. $49 all-in, delivered in one business hour.

## Where litigators lose the case before filing

**Trap 1: Wrong legal name.** Suing "ABC Consulting" when the legal name is "1234567 Ontario Inc. o/a ABC Consulting" invites a motion to strike. The rule: name the corporation exactly as the registry names it — including the numbered corporation designation if applicable — then add "o/a ABC Consulting" if the operating name is relevant.

**Trap 2: Serving at an outdated address.** Corporations move registered offices. If you serve at last year's address, service may be defective. Order a fresh Profile Report so you're serving at the current registered office as of the filing date.

**Trap 3: Suing a dissolved corporation.** If the corporation is struck or dissolved, it has no legal existence. The claim is a nullity. Check status *before* filing — not after the defendant's counsel points it out.

**Trap 4: Missing the director-liability window.** Director liability under the *Business Corporations Act*, environmental legislation, or CRA source-deductions rules attaches to individuals who were directors at the time of the wrongdoing. If you're pleading director liability, you need not just current directors but historical directors — that's a [Minute Book](/minute-books) search, not a Profile Report.

## Reading the Profile Report for litigation

Focus on:

- **Legal name** — exactly as it appears at the top of the report. Copy verbatim into pleadings.
- **Any operating names / trade names** — plead these if relevant.
- **Registered office address** — this is your service address unless provincial rules allow otherwise.
- **Status** — must be "Active." If not, adjust strategy.
- **Directors** — for statutory director-liability claims, these are your defendants (or additional defendants) at the time of the wrong.
- **Business Number** — useful for cross-referencing tax-related pleadings.

## What the Profile Report *doesn't* give you

For litigation involving historical facts (past directors during the events at issue, prior transactions, old amalgamations, share-transfer history), the Profile Report only shows *current* state. The corporation's [Minute Book](/minute-books) is the full record. If your matter turns on who was a director five years ago or what resolution was passed in 2019, you'll need Minute Book discovery — which is typically obtained through pleadings and civil discovery, not a $49 registry order.

## Sample litigation-diligence timeline

For a $150,000 breach-of-contract claim filed in Ontario:

1. **T-14 days (statement of claim drafting)** — Order Corporate Profile Report on defendant corporation ($49). Confirm legal name, registered office, status.
2. **T-10 days** — Draft pleadings using verified legal name and registered office. If pleading director liability, cross-check historical director timing.
3. **T-7 days (statement of claim finalised)** — Fresh Profile Report if the first pull is over 14 days old.
4. **Filing day** — Statement of claim issued. Registered office confirmed on Profile Report of same day.
5. **Service** — Process server or courier to registered office address. Attach the Profile Report to the affidavit of service.

## Small claims: same discipline, lower stakes

For a small-claims filing (BC Civil Resolution Tribunal, Ontario Small Claims Court, etc.), the corporate search rules apply the same way — you must name and serve the corporation correctly. The claim amount is smaller but the doctrine is identical. Order the Profile Report. It's cheap insurance on a claim you'd rather have go smoothly.

## Collections: verifying before you spend legal fees

Before spending $2,000+ on collections legal work, confirm the corporation still exists and has assets to pursue. If a Profile Report shows the corporation was struck six months ago, the collections file needs to shift focus: either revive the corporation and continue (rare, expensive), or pursue directors personally where statutory grounds exist.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I use a stale Profile Report for service?

Legally, service is judged against the registered office at the time of service. If you serve at an address that was the registered office last month but has since moved, the service can be challenged. Best practice: order the Profile Report dated within 14 days of filing/service.

### What if the corporation has multiple registered offices?

A corporation has exactly one *registered office* on the corporate registry at any given time. It may have several business locations, head offices, or branch offices — those are not "registered offices" for service purposes. Serve at the registered office on the Profile Report.

### Can I sue a corporation that has moved to another province?

Corporations that are registered extra-provincially in the province where the claim arises can be sued there. Check both the incorporating jurisdiction and the extra-provincial registration jurisdiction. Get the extra-provincial registration search from the province of dispute.

### The corporation was recently amalgamated. Who do I sue?

The surviving amalgamated entity generally inherits the liabilities of its predecessors. Sue the surviving entity, using its post-amalgamation legal name, and plead the amalgamation history in the statement of claim. Confirm the amalgamation from the Profile Report — it will note amalgamation status.
