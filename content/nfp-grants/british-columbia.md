# Grants for Nonprofits in British Columbia (2026 Funding Guide)

British Columbia is one of the better-funded provinces in Canada for community organizations, and most of the money flows through a handful of well-established channels. If you are searching for grants for nonprofits in British Columbia, the place to start is the provincial government's Community Gaming Grants program, which redistributes commercial gambling revenue to not-for-profit organizations across six sectors every year. Around it sit the BC Arts Council's project and operating streams, the Civil Forfeiture Grant program for community safety work, and event-focused funding delivered through the province's tourism programs.

Federal programs layer on top of all of this, and community foundations, municipalities and United Ways fill in local gaps. This guide covers the major provincial programs — verified amounts, intake windows and official links — along with recent 2026 funding announcements, so a first-time applicant can see the whole landscape on one page.

Community Gaming Grants are the workhorse of BC's not-for-profit funding system, and each sector has its own application window. For 2026 the published intakes are: arts and culture (February 1 – April 30), sport (March 1 – May 31), environment and public safety (July 1 – August 31), human and social services (August 1 – November 30), and parent advisory councils and district PACs (April 1 – June 30). Capital Project Grants accept applications from July 17 to August 31, with decisions by December 31. If you are reading this in mid-July 2026, the environment, public safety and capital project windows are open right now — full dates are on the [application page](https://www2.gov.bc.ca/gov/content/sports-culture/gambling-fundraising/gaming-grants/cggapply).

Note that Community Gaming Grants fund organizations that already deliver programs: the guidelines state that programs must be running at the time of application, and startup funding is not available. New organizations often begin with smaller local funders or project grants and graduate to gaming grants after their first year of operations.

## Who qualifies — and why incorporation matters

Almost every program above is restricted to organizations, not individuals — and in practice that means an incorporated not-for-profit. Community Gaming Grants, for example, require a not-for-profit organization delivering programs directly, with an open membership and a democratic process for electing a volunteer board. The BC Arts Council's organization streams fund established non-profit societies, and the Civil Forfeiture program funds community organizations rather than informal groups.

Funders also look past the certificate of incorporation. Expect to provide financial statements, proof of a functioning board, your constitution and bylaws (the purposes in your constitution determine which gaming grant sector you apply under), and often a year or more of program delivery. If your group is still unincorporated, incorporating as a society under BC's Societies Act — or federally — is the single most important step toward grant eligibility, because it creates the legal entity, governance structure and records that every application form asks about.

## How to strengthen your grant application

Strong applications in BC share a few habits. First, match the program before you write a word: read the current-year guidelines, confirm your organization type and sector, and check that your constitution's purposes align with what you are asking to fund. Gaming grant assessors specifically compare your request against your constitution.

Second, show financial need without showing financial chaos. Provide clean, board-approved financial statements and a realistic program budget where the grant fills a genuine gap. Third, quantify community benefit: how many people you serve, who they are, and what changes because of your program. Programs like the Civil Forfeiture Grants publish their priority streams each year — mirror that language honestly. Finally, gather letters of support from partner organizations and, for capital projects, confirm your matching funds early; BC's Capital Project Grants require applicants to demonstrate matching money is in place.

## Frequently asked questions

### What grants are available for nonprofits in British Columbia?

The largest provincial source is the Community Gaming Grants program, which funds not-for-profits in arts and culture, sport, environment, public safety, human and social services, and PACs — up to $125,000 a year for local organizations. The BC Arts Council, Civil Forfeiture Grant program and Destination Events Program add sector-specific funding, and federal programs stack on top.

### How much can a BC nonprofit get from Community Gaming Grants?

Local organizations can receive up to $125,000 per year and regional or provincial organizations up to $250,000 per year through Program Grants. Separate Capital Project Grants provide up to $250,000, covering 20–50% of an eligible capital project costing between $20,000 and $1,250,000, with matching funds required.

### When are BC Community Gaming Grant deadlines in 2026?

Each sector has its own window: arts and culture (February 1 – April 30), sport (March 1 – May 31), PACs and DPACs (April 1 – June 30), environment and public safety (July 1 – August 31), human and social services (August 1 – November 30), and capital projects (July 17 – August 31).

### Do you need to be a registered charity to get grants in BC?

No. Most provincial programs, including Community Gaming Grants, require incorporated not-for-profit status — not charitable registration. Charitable status with the CRA matters mainly for foundations that can only grant to qualified donees, and for issuing donation receipts. Many BC not-for-profits access provincial funding for years without ever registering as a charity.

### Can a new nonprofit get a gaming grant in BC?

Not immediately. Community Gaming Grants fund programs that are already running at the time of application, and the guidelines state that startup funding is not available. New organizations typically incorporate first, run a season of programming with local or project-based funding, then apply during their sector's next intake window.
