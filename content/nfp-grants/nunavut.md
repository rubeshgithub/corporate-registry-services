# Grants for Not-for-Profits in Nunavut (2026 Funding Guide)

Grants for nonprofits in Nunavut come from a funding landscape unlike anywhere else in Canada. The Government of Nunavut (GN) is the anchor funder: its Department of Culture and Heritage alone funds over 100 separate grants and contributions each year to community-based, non-profit organizations, individuals and municipal corporations, while the Department of Community and Government Services (CGS) runs the territory's sport and recreation funding. Alongside the GN, regional Inuit organizations — such as the Kakivak Association in the Qikiqtani region — deliver community economic development, childcare and training funding rooted in the land claims framework, and federal programs remain fully open to Nunavut applicants.

Most GN funding is distributed through annual calls for proposals rather than standing deadlines, so the rhythm of grant-seeking in Nunavut is watching for the call, being ready, and applying fast. Being "ready" almost always means being an incorporated non-profit in good standing with the territorial registry. Here is what was verified as of July 2026.

The Culture and Heritage envelope is broader than its name suggests. The department's 2025–26 call for proposals covered streams including Arts, Culture and Heritage Grants, Heritage Centre and Heritage Facilities Core Funding, Public Library Services Contributions, Elders' Initiatives Grants, Elders and Youth Committee Grants, Youth Initiative Grants, the Community Radio Grant, and French and Inuit language programming — meaning almost any community organization in the territory, from a hamlet radio society to a heritage centre, has a potential stream. Amounts differ by stream, so request the current program guidelines from the department's grants administrators when a call opens.

Sport and recreation funding runs on a similar annual cycle: CGS's Sport and Recreation Division issues a call for grant and contribution applications each year (the most recent was announced in December 2025 for the coming fiscal year), open to Nunavut municipalities and Nunavut non-profit organizations in good standing with the legal registries.

For organizations in the Qikiqtani region, the Kakivak Association is a funder worth knowing well. As the region's Inuit community economic development organization, it funds childcare centres, supports employers and training partners, and backs students, youth and artists — and its staff explicitly help applicants identify the right program. Baffin-region non-profits running daycares or training programs frequently combine Kakivak support with GN funding. Similar regional Inuit organizations serve the Kivalliq and Kitikmeot regions.

## Federal grants Nunavut not-for-profits can also access

Federal programs materially expand what a Nunavut organization can do. [Canada Summer Jobs](https://www.canada.ca/en/employment-social-development/services/funding/canada-summer-jobs.html) reimburses not-for-profit employers up to 100% of the territorial adult minimum hourly wage plus mandatory employment-related costs — a full wage subsidy for summer youth positions. The [New Horizons for Seniors Program](https://www.canada.ca/en/employment-social-development/services/funding/new-horizons-seniors-community-based.html) funds community-based projects for Elders and seniors up to $50,000; its 2026 call for proposals ran June 2 to July 14, 2026, and calls are typically annual. National arts, language and heritage funds also prioritize Indigenous-led and northern projects. Our [federal grants guide](/nfp-grants/federal) covers the full list.

## Who qualifies — and why incorporation matters

Across GN programs, the eligible-applicant list reads the same way: community-based non-profit organizations, municipal corporations, and in some streams individuals. The sport and recreation program states it plainly — applicants must be Nunavut non-profit organizations "in good standing with legal registries." In practice that means incorporation under Nunavut's Societies Act (or another recognized statute), current annual filings, and a board the GN can hold accountable through a contribution agreement.

For larger contributions — the $100,000 Inuit Societal Values awards, heritage facility core funding, library services — the GN is entering multi-installment funding relationships with reporting obligations, and it will not do that with an unincorporated committee. Incorporation also matters on the Inuit-organization side: a registered society with clear governance is a stronger partner for Kakivak or a regional Inuit association than an informal group. If your organization has not yet incorporated, see our step-by-step guide to [incorporating a not-for-profit in Nunavut](/not-for-profit/nunavut) — doing it before the next call for proposals opens means you can apply the day the call is announced rather than scrambling to register mid-window.

## How to strengthen your grant application

Because GN funding moves through time-limited calls, preparation beats reaction. Keep a standing "grant kit": certificate of incorporation, current registry filings, board list, most recent financial statements and a one-page organizational profile. When a call opens — sometimes with only weeks before the deadline — you then only need to write the project itself.

Root the proposal in community priorities and Inuit societal values. Culture and Heritage explicitly funds projects that strengthen the role of Elders, and proposals that involve Elders in planning and delivery — not just as participants — align with how the department assesses Inuit Societal Values funding. Budget honestly for northern realities: charter travel, freight and seasonal windows are understood by GN reviewers, but they expect to see them costed, not discovered later. Line up letters of support from your hamlet council, local Elders' committee or regional Inuit organization. And report on time: achievement reports comparing actual results to what you proposed are a stated condition of Culture and Heritage funding, and a clean reporting record is your strongest asset in the next round.

## Frequently asked questions

### What grants are available for nonprofits in Nunavut?

The Government of Nunavut's Department of Culture and Heritage funds over 100 grants and contributions annually across arts, heritage, Elders', youth, language and community radio streams, plus Inuit Societal Values funding up to $100,000. Community and Government Services runs annual sport and recreation funding, and regional Inuit organizations like the Kakivak Association fund childcare, training and community economic development.

### Do you need to be a registered charity to get grants in Nunavut?

No. GN programs fund community-based non-profit organizations and municipal corporations without requiring CRA charitable registration. What matters is being incorporated and in good standing with the territorial legal registries. Charitable status becomes relevant only if you want to issue donation tax receipts or access certain foundation funders.

### How do Nunavut government grant deadlines work?

Most GN funding is released through annual calls for proposals rather than fixed recurring deadlines. Culture and Heritage typically issues its call for the fiscal year (with a second call if budget remains — the 2025–26 second call closed June 20, 2025), and CGS issues a sport and recreation call each year, most recently in December 2025. Watch the GN newsroom and department pages.

### What is the maximum Inuit Societal Values grant?

The Department of Culture and Heritage can award up to $100,000 for projects that strengthen the role of Elders and promote community healing through Inuit values. Projects must involve Elders in planning and delivery, payments are made in two installments, and recipients must submit reports comparing actual outcomes to their proposal.

### What funding does the Kakivak Association offer?

Kakivak is the community economic development organization serving Inuit in the Qikiqtani region. It funds childcare centres, students and youth (post-secondary and training), employers and training partners, job seekers, artists and Inuit-owned businesses. Amounts vary by program; Kakivak's support staff help applicants identify the right fit. Equivalent organizations serve other Nunavut regions.
