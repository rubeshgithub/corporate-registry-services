# How to Start a Not-for-Profit in Canada: The Complete Incorporation Guide

Every hockey association, food bank, cultural society, and community league in Canada started the same way: a group of people with a mission, staring at government forms. This guide explains what a not-for-profit actually is in Canadian law, why incorporating one is almost always worth it, and how the process works in each of Canada's 14 jurisdictions — one federal regime and thirteen provincial and territorial ones, each with its own act, forms, fees, and board rules.

## What is a not-for-profit organization?

A not-for-profit (also written non-profit or NFP) is an organization that exists to advance a purpose — community benefit, sport, culture, religion, education, advocacy — rather than to enrich its members. The defining legal feature is the **non-distribution constraint**: a not-for-profit can earn revenue, hold property, hire staff, and even run surpluses, but it cannot pay out profits to members the way a business corporation pays dividends to shareholders. Any gains must be recycled into the organization's purposes.

In Canada, not-for-profits are creatures of statute, and the statute depends on where you incorporate. Federally, it's the *Canada Not-for-profit Corporations Act* (CNCA). Provincially, it's a patchwork: **Societies Acts** in British Columbia, Alberta, Nova Scotia, Yukon, the Northwest Territories, and Nunavut; **Not-for-Profit or Non-profit Corporations Acts** in Ontario (ONCA) and Saskatchewan; **Companies Act** regimes in Quebec (Part III), New Brunswick (Part II), and Prince Edward Island (Part II); and **Corporations Act** non-share provisions in Manitoba and Newfoundland and Labrador.

### Not-for-profit vs. registered charity — they are not the same thing

This is the single most common confusion. Incorporation creates the legal entity; **charitable registration** is a separate, later step with the Canada Revenue Agency that lets you issue donation tax receipts and access charity-only funding. Most registered charities are incorporated not-for-profits, but most not-for-profits are not charities. If charity status is part of your plan, your incorporation documents — especially your purposes clause — need to be drafted with the CRA's requirements in mind from day one.

## Why incorporate instead of staying an informal group?

An unincorporated association is legally just its members — which means the members personally carry its risks. Incorporation creates a separate legal person, and with it four practical advantages. **Limited liability:** contracts, leases, and debts belong to the corporation, not the volunteers who signed up to help. **Perpetual existence:** the organization survives changes in its board and membership, which matters for property, long-term programs, and institutional memory. **Credibility and money:** banks require incorporation documents to open an organizational account, and nearly every government grant program in Canada — see our [grants guides](/nfp-grants/) — requires applicants to be incorporated not-for-profits in good standing. **A path to charity status:** the CRA registers corporations, not campfire agreements.

## Federal or provincial: which incorporation is right for you?

Incorporating **federally under the CNCA** gives you name protection across Canada, a modern flexible statute, and a structure funders recognize everywhere — the natural choice for organizations that will operate in more than one province. It requires as little as one director, though a *soliciting* corporation (one that asks the public for money) needs at least three. Note that federal corporations must still register extra-provincially where they operate.

Incorporating **provincially** keeps everything in one registry, is often cheaper (Alberta's fee is about $51.50; Nova Scotia's about $43.60), and fits organizations rooted in one place — a community hall, a local league, a provincial association. The trade-offs are name protection limited to your province and board rules that vary widely: three directors minimum in Ontario, Quebec, Manitoba, New Brunswick, PEI, and Newfoundland and Labrador; five members or incorporators to even file in Alberta, Nova Scotia, the NWT, and Nunavut; special low-requirement categories like BC's and Yukon's member-funded societies.

## The incorporation process, at 10,000 feet

Details vary by jurisdiction, but every Canadian not-for-profit incorporation follows the same arc. First, **choose and clear a name** — through NUANS federally and in several provinces, or the provincial registry's own search — and have backups ready, because conflicts are the most common cause of rejection. Second, **draft your constitution documents**: articles of incorporation or a memorandum/application, a purposes or objects clause, and bylaws governing members, directors, meetings, and finances. Third, **assemble the minimum board and members** your statute requires and gather each person's legal name and address. Fourth, **file with the registry** — online in most jurisdictions now — with the fee, a registered office address, and your director notices. Finally, **stay compliant**: file the annual return or report every year, hold your AGM, and keep your registers current. Registries dissolve corporations that go quiet; several strike organizations after as little as two missed years.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the difference between a not-for-profit and a non-profit?

Nothing — the terms are interchangeable in Canada. Statutes vary in wording: the federal act and Ontario's say "not-for-profit," Saskatchewan's says "non-profit," and six jurisdictions call the incorporated entity a "society." All describe the same idea: a corporation without share capital that cannot distribute gains to its members.

### How much does it cost to incorporate a not-for-profit in Canada?

Government fees range from about $43.60 in Nova Scotia and $50–$70 in the territories, Saskatchewan and Newfoundland and Labrador, to $155 in Ontario, $200 federally (online), and about $199 in Quebec — plus name-search costs where NUANS or reservation applies. Professional preparation, bylaws, and minute books are separate.

### How many people do I need to start a not-for-profit?

It ranges from one person (federal, BC, Saskatchewan membership corporations, NL) to three (Ontario, Quebec, NB, PEI, Yukon) to five members or incorporators (Alberta, Nova Scotia, NWT, Nunavut). Most jurisdictions also set a minimum board — commonly three directors — and federal soliciting corporations need three.

### Can a not-for-profit make money in Canada?

Yes. A not-for-profit can charge fees, sell goods and services related to its purposes, and end the year with a surplus. What it cannot do is distribute that surplus to members as profit. Significant unrelated commercial activity can also jeopardize tax-exempt status — structure matters, which is worth professional advice.

### Do I need a lawyer to incorporate a not-for-profit?

No — every registry accepts filings directly from founders. But purposes clauses, bylaws, and the federal-vs-provincial choice have long-term consequences, especially if you'll seek charitable registration or grants.
