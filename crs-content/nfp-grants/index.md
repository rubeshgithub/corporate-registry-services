# Grants for Not-for-Profits in Canada: The Complete Funding Guide

Billions of dollars flow to Canadian not-for-profits every year — from federal departments, provincial ministries, lottery and gaming revenues, arts councils, and community foundations. The money is real, but it is scattered across dozens of programs with different eligibility rules, intake windows, and paperwork. This guide is the map: what's available federally, what each province and territory offers, and — just as important — what funders require before they'll write a cheque.

Each jurisdiction below has its own detailed guide, refreshed for the 2026 funding cycles, with program amounts, deadlines, links to the official application pages, and recent award announcements so you can see who is actually getting funded.

## Where the money comes from

Not-for-profit funding in Canada flows through four broad channels. **Federal programs** run by departments like Employment and Social Development Canada and Canadian Heritage fund national priorities — youth employment (Canada Summer Jobs), seniors (New Horizons for Seniors, up to $50,000), accessibility (Enabling Accessibility Fund), arts and cultural spaces. **Provincial and territorial programs** fund community infrastructure, sport, culture, and social services — flagships like the Ontario Trillium Foundation (grants up to $600,000 over multiple years), BC's Community Gaming Grants (up to $250,000 a year), and Alberta's CFEP and CIP. **Lottery and gaming revenue** underwrites many of those provincial streams, from Sask Lotteries to Lotteries Yukon. And **foundations and municipalities** layer local money on top — often the easiest first grant for a young organization.

## What almost every grant program requires

Read ten Canadian grant guidelines and the same eligibility list appears in nine of them. Applicants must be **incorporated not-for-profits in good standing** — a society, non-share corporation, or CNCA corporation, with the registry number to prove it and annual returns filed on time. Most ask for **governance evidence**: a list of directors, bylaws, minutes of the meeting authorizing the application. Many ask for **financial statements** — sometimes reviewed or audited above revenue thresholds — and a **project budget** with confirmed matching contributions where cost-sharing applies. Some, like Saskatchewan's Community Initiatives Fund, state the incorporation requirement explicitly; others enforce it quietly at the review stage.

The practical consequence: the time to incorporate is *before* the intake window opens, not during it. Registration takes days to weeks depending on the jurisdiction, and a certificate dated after the deadline helps nobody. If you're not yet incorporated, start with our [incorporation guides](/not-for-profit/) — then come back for the money.

## How to win: what separates funded applications

Funders repeat the same advice in their own guidelines and award announcements. Match the program's purpose precisely — a capital program funds buildings and equipment, not salaries, and reviewers reject mismatches first. Show community need with numbers and letters of support, not adjectives. Budget honestly, including your own contribution; cost-shared programs like CFEP (50% matching) and PEI's Rural Growth Initiative (50–75%) require it. Demonstrate capacity: a functioning board, clean filings, and financials signal you can deliver and report. And plan for outcomes — most programs now require a final report with measurable results, and your next application depends on the last one being clean.

## Frequently asked questions

### What grants are available for nonprofits in Canada?

Federal programs (Canada Summer Jobs, New Horizons for Seniors up to $50,000, Enabling Accessibility Fund up to $125,000 for small projects), provincial flagships (Ontario Trillium Foundation up to $600,000 multi-year, BC Community Gaming Grants up to $250,000/year, Alberta CFEP up to $1M), plus arts councils, lottery-funded streams, and municipal and foundation grants in every region.

### Do you need to be a registered charity to get grants in Canada?

Usually no — most federal and provincial programs fund incorporated not-for-profits whether or not they hold CRA charitable registration. Some private foundations can only grant to "qualified donees" (mostly registered charities), so charity status widens the pool, but incorporation in good standing is the near-universal baseline requirement.

### Can a new nonprofit get grants?

Yes, though options narrow. Some programs require a year of operations or past financial statements, but many project-based streams — community initiative funds, small arts and events grants, wellness grants — fund newly incorporated organizations with a solid board, clear budget, and demonstrated community need. Municipal and foundation micro-grants are common first wins.

### How long does a grant application take?

Plan backwards from the deadline: incorporation (days to weeks if not already done), board resolution, letters of support, and budget can take a month; the funder's decision typically takes eight to sixteen weeks after the intake closes. Programs with fixed windows (OTF, Community Gaming Grants) publish decision dates in their guidelines.

### Do grants cover operating costs or only projects?

Both exist, but operating (core) funding is scarcer and often closed to new applicants — examples include BC Arts Council Operating Assistance and NWT's NGO Stabilization Fund. Most first grants are project- or capital-based. Read each program's eligible-costs list carefully; staff time tied to a funded project is often claimable even in project grants.
