# Grants for Not-for-Profits in Nova Scotia (2026 Funding Guide)

Community halls, rinks, festivals, arts groups and heritage societies keep Nova Scotia's towns alive — and the province funds them accordingly. Most grants for nonprofits in Nova Scotia flow through the Department of Communities, Culture, Tourism and Heritage (CCTH), which runs an annual suite of capital and project programs, and through Arts Nova Scotia, the arm's-length agency that funds professional arts activity. A distinctive Nova Scotia twist is **Support4Culture**, a designated lottery program: proceeds from designated lottery products sold across the province are directed into arts, culture and heritage funding administered by CCTH and Arts Nova Scotia ([support4culture.ca](https://support4culture.ca/about/)).

The rhythm of provincial funding here is predictable once you know it. Most CCTH infrastructure programs open their applications in December and close in mid-February, with cheques flowing over the spring and summer — in June 2026 the province confirmed the next application period for its facility programs opens in December 2026 ([news release, June 25, 2026](https://news.novascotia.ca/en/2026/06/25/province-invests-community-recreation-facilities)). That gives new organizations a clear runway: incorporate, organize your board and financials through the autumn, and be ready when the portal opens.

Eligibility across the CCTH suite consistently favours organized, incorporated bodies: CFIP is open to non-profit organizations, registered charities and First Nations that operate primarily for community, culture and diversity and either own their facility or hold a lease of at least five years; the recreation and accessibility programs likewise serve community groups, municipalities and other not-for-profit organizations. Arts Nova Scotia's organizational grants go to non-profit professional arts organizations registered in Nova Scotia. Support4Culture-funded streams such as the Diversity and Community Capacity Fund require applicants to be registered, Nova Scotia-based non-profit societies or cooperatives in good standing — wording that makes your Registry of Joint Stock Companies status a live issue at application time. CCTH's full program list is on its [Investing in our Future page](https://cch.novascotia.ca/investing-in-our-future), and the province maintains a [searchable grants directory](https://www.novascotia.ca/find-grants-offered-through-department-communities-culture-tourism-and-heritage).

## Who qualifies for grants for nonprofits in Nova Scotia — and why incorporation matters

Read any CCTH or Arts Nova Scotia guideline and the same threshold appears: the applicant must be a legally constituted not-for-profit in good standing. In Nova Scotia that usually means a society incorporated under the Societies Act and registered with the Registry of Joint Stock Companies, with its annual renewals up to date — "good standing" is not a figure of speech, and a lapsed registration can sink an otherwise strong application. Capital programs add further tests that only an incorporated body can meet, such as owning the facility or holding a five-year lease in the organization's own name, carrying insurance, and signing a contribution agreement.

Funders also expect the practical trappings of incorporation: a board of directors, bylaws, an annual general meeting, and financial statements. ## How to strengthen your grant application

Nova Scotia's capital programs are cost-shared, so the strongest applications arrive with the applicant's share already secured — bank statements, council resolutions or confirmed pledges covering your one-third (recreation, accessibility) or one-quarter (CFIP) portion. Get contractor quotes early; February deadlines mean pricing work in the dead of winter, and vague estimates read as risk. Show that the facility genuinely serves the public: usage numbers, rental calendars and letters from user groups all help reviewers see community benefit rather than private advantage.

For Arts Nova Scotia, remember that peer assessors judge artistic merit first — invest in your artistic dossier, budget realistic artist fees, and apply to the correct category. Across all programs, tidy governance is quiet persuasion: an up-to-date Registry of Joint Stock Companies profile, current financial statements and a stable board tell a funder their money will be accounted for. Finally, talk to program officers before applying — CCTH explicitly invites it, and a fifteen-minute call can save a wasted intake.

## Frequently asked questions

### What grants are available for nonprofits in Nova Scotia?

The core provincial suite comes from Communities, Culture, Tourism and Heritage: the Community Facilities Improvement Program for building repairs, the Recreation Facility Development Grant and Rink Revitalization Fund for recreation capital (up to $150,000), and the Community ACCESS-Ability Program for accessibility. Arts Nova Scotia funds arts organizations, and Support4Culture lottery proceeds fund diversity and culture streams.

### How much does the Community Facilities Improvement Program pay?

In recent intakes, CFIP provided grants of up to $50,000, covering up to 75 per cent of eligible project costs for repairs and improvements to existing community facilities. Applicants contribute the remaining share and must own the building or hold a lease of at least five years. Always confirm amounts in the current year's guidelines before budgeting.

### When can I apply for Nova Scotia community facility grants?

The province's facility and recreation programs run on an annual cycle: applications typically open in December and close in mid-February. Following the June 2026 funding announcement, the province confirmed the next application period opens in December 2026. Preparing your incorporation, quotes and matching funds during the autumn puts you in the strongest position.

### What is Support4Culture in Nova Scotia?

Support4Culture is a designated lottery program: proceeds from designated lottery products sold in Nova Scotia are directed to arts, culture and heritage funding. It supports a family of programs administered by CCTH and Arts Nova Scotia, including the Diversity and Community Capacity Fund, the Mi'kmaq Arts Program and the Arts Equity Funding Initiative.

### Do you need to be a registered charity to get grants in Nova Scotia?

No — most provincial programs require incorporated not-for-profit status rather than charitable registration. CFIP, for example, accepts non-profit organizations, registered charities and First Nations. What matters is being a legally constituted society in good standing with the Registry of Joint Stock Companies, with a board, bylaws and financial statements funders can rely on.

### How much can a nonprofit get for a recreation facility in Nova Scotia?

The Recreation Facility Development Grant covers up to two-thirds of a project's total capital cost, to a maximum of $150,000 per project, for facilities such as pools, arenas, fields, trails and playgrounds. The Rink Revitalization Fund offers the same parameters for rink and arena upgrades. Both fund not-for-profit organizations, community groups and municipalities.
