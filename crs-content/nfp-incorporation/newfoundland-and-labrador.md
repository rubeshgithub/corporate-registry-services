# How to Incorporate a Not-for-Profit in Newfoundland and Labrador (Corporations Act Guide)

If you are wondering how to incorporate a not-for-profit in Newfoundland and Labrador, the answer starts with the province's [Corporations Act, RSNL 1990, c. C-36](https://www.assembly.nl.ca/legislation/sr/statutes/c36.htm). Unlike most provinces and territories, Newfoundland and Labrador has no separate societies statute: community organizations incorporate under Part XXI of the Corporations Act as "corporations without share capital." Filings go to the provincial [Registry of Companies](https://www.gov.nl.ca/gs/registries/companies/corp-inc/), the Government Services registry operated by the Government of Newfoundland and Labrador (Digital Government and Service NL).

This guide is written for first-time incorporators — the volunteers behind sports clubs, cultural associations, church groups, community leagues and prospective charities across the province. It walks through the legal requirements, the forms, the fees, and what you must file every year to stay in good standing.

## What counts as a not-for-profit in Newfoundland and Labrador

Under Part XXI of the Corporations Act, a not-for-profit is a **corporation without share capital**. It has members instead of shareholders, and because there are no shares, there is no mechanism for distributing profits to the people who control it. Any surplus the organization earns must be applied to its objects — the purposes set out in its articles — rather than paid out as dividends. That non-distribution constraint is the legal line between a not-for-profit corporation and a for-profit company incorporated under the same Act with share capital.

It is equally important to understand what incorporation does *not* do. Incorporating provincially does not make your organization a **registered charity**: charitable status is granted separately by the Canada Revenue Agency under the federal *Income Tax Act*, and only registered charities can issue official donation receipts. Many NL organizations incorporate under Part XXI first, then apply to the CRA once their governing documents and charitable purposes are in order. The [Registry of Companies incorporation page](https://www.gov.nl.ca/gs/registries/companies/corp-inc/) notes this distinction and points charitable applicants to the CRA.

## Why incorporate your not-for-profit

An unincorporated club or association is legally just its members, which means those members can be personally exposed if something goes wrong. Incorporation creates a separate legal person. The corporation — not the volunteers — signs the lease, holds the bank account, owns the equipment and carries the contracts. Directors and members get the protection of **limited liability**, and the organization gains **perpetual existence**, carrying on smoothly as founders retire and new boards are elected.

Incorporation also builds credibility where it counts. Funders, government grant programs and financial institutions routinely ask for a certificate of incorporation, a registered office address and proof of good standing before they will advance money or open accounts. An incorporated body can hold property in its own name, and a Part XXI corporation with exclusively charitable purposes has the corporate foundation the CRA expects to see in a charitable registration application.

## Step-by-step: how to incorporate

1. **Choose and clear your name.** Newfoundland and Labrador does not use the NUANS system; the Registry of Companies runs its own corporate name approval process. You can research names by phone at (709) 729-3317 or search online, then reserve your proposed name for a non-refundable **$10** fee. A reservation holds the name for **90 days** and can be renewed twice. Name reservation is mandatory if you plan to incorporate online. Corporate names generally carry a legal element such as "Limited," "Corporation" or "Inc." — confirm the exact styling of your not-for-profit name with the Registry during approval.
2. **Prepare Articles of Incorporation — Form 1a.** Corporations without share capital use [Form 1a, Articles of Incorporation Without Share Capital](https://www.gov.nl.ca/gs/files/forms-files-corp01a-art-inc.pdf), together with the Schedule A and Schedule B attachments the Registry requires for Part XXI corporations. The articles are submitted by one or more incorporators; a corporate incorporator must itself be in good standing.
3. **Assemble your first board.** Not-for-profit entities in the province must have a **minimum of three directors**, and every director must be at least 19 years old. List them on [Form 6, Notice of Directors](https://www.gov.nl.ca/gs/files/forms-files-corp06-note-dir.pdf).
4. **Fix a registered office in the province.** The registered office is the location within Newfoundland and Labrador where corporate records are kept. It needs a full civic address — a mailbox service is not acceptable. File it on [Form 3, Notice of Registered Office](https://www.gov.nl.ca/gs/files/forms-files-corp03-note-reg-off.pdf).
5. **File and pay the fee.** Local incorporations can be filed online through the Registry's CADO eServices system or on paper. Under the [schedule of fees](https://www.gov.nl.ca/gs/files/registries-companies-fees-corp.pdf), a Part XXI certificate of incorporation costs **$70 on paper or $63 filed electronically** — a fraction of the $300 fee for a share-capital company.
6. **Receive your certificate of incorporation.** Once the Registry accepts the filing, your organization exists as a corporation without share capital. Keep the certificate with your minute book; funders and banks will ask for it.

## After incorporation: ongoing compliance

Every NL corporation, including a corporation without share capital, must file an **annual return** to keep its status. The [Registry's annual returns guidance](https://www.gov.nl.ca/gs/registries/companies/annual-returns/) requires the return to be filed before the end of the month in which the anniversary of registration, amalgamation or revival falls; corporations without share capital use **Form 29**. The good news for not-for-profits: under the current fee schedule, the annual return for a Part XXI corporation carries **no government fee**, and returns can be filed online through the CADO eServices system around the clock.

Missing the deadline puts the corporation into "Not in Good Standing" status — a red flag for funders and lenders — and a corporation that neglects its returns for **three years can be involuntarily dissolved and struck from the registry**. Alongside the annual return, keep your registered office and director information current (Forms 3 and 6), hold your annual members' meeting as your by-laws require, and maintain your minute book, member register and financial records. If your articles ever need updating, articles of amendment for a Part XXI corporation are currently filed without a fee.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many board members do I need for a nonprofit in Newfoundland and Labrador?

Three. The Registry of Companies requires not-for-profit entities — corporations without share capital under Part XXI of the Corporations Act — to have a minimum of three directors, and each director must be at least 19 years old. List your first board on Form 6, Notice of Directors, when you file your articles of incorporation.

### How much does it cost to incorporate a not-for-profit in Newfoundland and Labrador?

Under the current Corporations Act fee schedule, a certificate of incorporation for a Part XXI corporation without share capital costs $70 on paper or $63 filed electronically, plus a $10 non-refundable name reservation. By comparison, a for-profit incorporation costs $300 — and the not-for-profit's annual return carries no government fee.

### Does Newfoundland and Labrador have a Societies Act?

No. Unlike British Columbia, Alberta or the territories, Newfoundland and Labrador has no separate societies statute. Not-for-profit organizations incorporate under Part XXI of the Corporations Act, RSNL 1990, c. C-36, as corporations without share capital, using Form 1a and filing with the provincial Registry of Companies.

### Do NL not-for-profit corporations have to file an annual return?

Yes. Every corporation without share capital must file an annual return (Form 29) before the end of its anniversary month each year. There is no government fee for Part XXI corporations, and filing is available online through CADO. Non-filing leads to "Not in Good Standing" status and, after three years, involuntary dissolution.

### Is an incorporated not-for-profit the same as a registered charity?

No. Provincial incorporation creates the legal entity; charitable registration is a separate approval from the Canada Revenue Agency under the Income Tax Act. Only a CRA-registered charity can issue official donation receipts. Most organizations incorporate first, then apply to the CRA with purposes drafted to meet the charity rules.

### Can a not-for-profit make a profit in Canada?

Yes — it can earn surpluses from memberships, fundraising or services. What it cannot do is distribute those profits to members or directors. A corporation without share capital has no shareholders, so every dollar of surplus must be applied to the organization's stated objects rather than paid out personally.
