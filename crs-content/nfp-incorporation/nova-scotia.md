# Nova Scotia Societies Act Incorporation: How to Start a Not-for-Profit Society

If you are launching a community group, sports club, cultural association or faith-based organization in Nova Scotia, the standard route is Nova Scotia Societies Act incorporation through the Registry of Joint Stock Companies (RJSC). A society is the province's not-for-profit corporate form: a legal entity created by five or more people for a purpose that benefits the community rather than its members' wallets.

The Registry of Joint Stock Companies, part of Service Nova Scotia, reviews your name, accepts your incorporation documents and keeps your society in good standing year after year. This guide is written for first-time incorporators — volunteers who have a mission and need a clear, plain-English map of the forms, fees and ongoing obligations.

## What counts as a not-for-profit in Nova Scotia

Under the Societies Act, any five or more persons who want to incorporate a society may subscribe to a Memorandum of Association and apply to the Registrar of Joint Stock Companies. The catch — and it is an important one — is purpose. A society must exist for the benefit of the community: charitable, educational, religious, recreational and similar aims all qualify. A society cannot be incorporated "for the purpose of carrying on any trade, industry or business," and its Memorandum must state plainly that it will not carry on a business.

That restriction is the non-distribution constraint in action. A society may charge membership fees, fundraise and even end a year with money in the bank, but surpluses must be reinvested in the society's objects, never distributed to members. Members' liability is limited to unpaid fees or dues.

Two distinctions matter for planning. First, a society is not automatically a registered charity — charitable registration is a separate application to the Canada Revenue Agency, and only registered charities can issue donation tax receipts. Second, a society differs from a for-profit company: there are no shares, no shareholders and no dividends. If your venture is really a business, Nova Scotia's company legislation, not the Societies Act, is the right home for it.

## Why incorporate your not-for-profit

Plenty of Nova Scotia groups operate for years as unincorporated associations, but incorporation changes the legal picture in ways funders and banks notice. An incorporated society is a legal person: it can sign leases, hold property, open bank accounts and enter contracts in its own name. Members enjoy limited liability, so a claim against the society does not normally reach personal assets — though directors should remember they can face personal liability in some situations, which is a good argument for clear by-laws and diligent governance.

Incorporation also gives your organization perpetual existence. Founders can move on, boards can turn over, and the society carries on. Just as practically, most provincial and federal grant programs, municipal funding streams and community foundations require applicants to be incorporated not-for-profits, often with a year or more of financial statements. And if a registered charity is in your future, the CRA will expect a properly constituted corporate body with governing documents that match charitable purposes. Incorporating under the Societies Act is the natural first step on that path.

## Step-by-step: Nova Scotia Societies Act incorporation

1. **Reserve your society's name.** Submit a name reservation request to the Registry of Joint Stock Companies before filing anything else. Name reservation is free for societies, and the Registry must approve the name before you can incorporate. Have alternatives ready in case your first choice conflicts with an existing name.
2. **Gather your five subscribers.** The Societies Act requires at least five people to make and subscribe the Memorandum of Association. Each subscriber's name, address and occupation appears on the subscriber list, with handwritten signatures witnessed by someone who is not a subscriber.
3. **Prepare the Memorandum of Association.** This founding document sets out your society's objects — the community-benefit purposes it will pursue — and includes the required statement that the society will not carry on a business.
4. **Draft your by-laws.** By-laws govern membership rules, how directors are appointed and what they do, officer roles, meeting procedures and financial reporting. Remember the principle the province itself emphasizes: the board manages the society, but it remains accountable to the members.
5. **Complete the supporting forms.** The incorporation package includes the Society Classification form, Notice of Directors and Officers, Notice of Registered Office and Appointment of Recognized Agent. Your registered office and your recognized agent — the individual who receives official notices — must both be in Nova Scotia.
6. **File and pay the $43.60 fee.** You can file online through the Registry's portal (uploading signed PDFs), mail the paper Society Incorporation Forms Package, or drop documents off at the Registry of Joint Stock Companies or an Access Nova Scotia location.
7. **Receive your certificate.** Expect your Certificate of Incorporation and CRA Business Number within one to two weeks, provided the Registry doesn't need corrections or more information.

## After incorporation: ongoing compliance

Incorporation is the beginning of a compliance rhythm, not the end of paperwork. Every society must hold an annual general meeting in Nova Scotia, and each year it must renew its registration with the RJSC. The renewal costs $31.15 and must be accompanied by the current list of directors coming out of the AGM and the society's most recent financial statements — a balance sheet and income statement signed by an auditor or by two directors.

Between renewals, keep the registry current: changes to your recognized agent, registered office, directors or officers' addresses all need to be filed. At the registered office itself, maintain your registers of members and directors along with minutes of every members' and directors' meeting. Fundamental changes — amending your objects or by-laws, for instance — require special resolutions filed with the Registry.

The consequence of neglect is serious. Under the Societies Act, a society that fails for two consecutive years to file a required return, notice or document, or that falls into arrears on fees, can be struck from the register — effectively dissolving it. Reinstating a struck society costs more time and money than simply renewing on schedule.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many people do you need to incorporate a society in Nova Scotia?

You need at least five people. The Societies Act requires a minimum of five subscribers to sign the Memorandum of Association, witnessed by a person who is not a subscriber. These five founding members typically also serve as the society's first directors, though your by-laws determine board composition going forward.

### How much does it cost to register a society in Nova Scotia?

The government incorporation fee is $43.60, and name reservation is free for societies. After incorporation, budget $31.15 for the annual renewal each year. Professional fees for preparing your Memorandum of Association and by-laws are extra if you use a service provider, so the do-it-yourself cost is under $50.

### How many board members does a Nova Scotia society need?

The Societies Act does not fix a minimum number of directors — your by-laws set the size of the board. In practice, most societies start with the five subscribers acting as directors, and at least one director or officer must be available to sign registry forms. Grant funders often prefer boards of three or more.

### Can a Nova Scotia society run a business?

No. A society cannot be incorporated to carry on a trade, industry or business, and its Memorandum of Association must say so. It can, however, charge membership fees, fundraise and earn revenue that supports its community purposes, provided surpluses are reinvested in the society's objects and never distributed to members.

### What does a society file with the RJSC every year?

Each year a society files its annual renewal ($31.15), the updated list of directors elected at its annual general meeting, and its latest financial statements — a balance sheet and income statement signed by an auditor or two directors. Missing filings for two consecutive years can lead to the society being struck from the register.

### Is a Nova Scotia society the same as a registered charity?

No. Incorporating under the Societies Act creates a provincial not-for-profit corporation, but charitable status is granted separately by the Canada Revenue Agency. Only a registered charity can issue official donation receipts. Many groups incorporate as a society first, then apply to the CRA once their purposes and governance documents are charity-ready.
