# How to Incorporate a Not-for-Profit in Ontario (ONCA Guide)

ONCA not-for-profit incorporation is governed by the Not-for-Profit Corporations Act, 2010 (ONCA) — Ontario's modern framework for corporations without share capital, in force since October 19, 2021. Under ONCA, a not-for-profit corporation exists to pursue its purposes rather than to enrich its members: any profit must be used for the organization's activities, never distributed to members, directors, or officers. Filings are made with ServiceOntario through the Ontario Business Registry.

This guide is for founders incorporating in Ontario for the first time — community associations, sports clubs, arts and cultural groups, faith communities, and organizations that may later seek charitable registration with the Canada Revenue Agency.

## What counts as a not-for-profit in Ontario

Under ONCA, a not-for-profit is a corporation without share capital: it has members instead of shareholders, and it operates under a strict non-distribution constraint — it may not distribute profits to its members, directors, or officers, and must use any surplus exclusively for its not-for-profit purposes. Earning revenue is fine; paying it out to insiders is not.

ONCA also distinguishes **public benefit corporations** — charitable corporations, plus non-charitable corporations that receive more than a threshold amount of public money — from other not-for-profits, and applies stronger financial-review rules to them (see the compliance section below). A separate distinction matters at the federal level: an Ontario not-for-profit is not automatically a **registered charity**. Charitable status comes only from the Canada Revenue Agency, and only registered charities can issue donation tax receipts. Many Ontario organizations incorporate under ONCA first and apply to the CRA afterwards.

On transition history: ONCA came into force on October 19, 2021 and gave existing Ontario not-for-profits three years — until October 18, 2024 — to update their governing documents. New incorporations are created directly under ONCA, so the rules in this guide apply from day one.

## Why incorporate your not-for-profit

An unincorporated club or association has no legal existence apart from its members, which leaves organizers personally exposed on leases, contracts, and debts. Incorporating under ONCA creates a separate legal entity: members are generally not personally responsible for the corporation's obligations, and the organization gains **perpetual existence** instead of dissolving whenever its founders move on.

Incorporation is also the gateway to funding. Grant programs — from provincial ministries to the Ontario Trillium Foundation and community foundations — overwhelmingly fund incorporated organizations, and routinely ask for a certificate of incorporation, corporate records, and evidence of board governance. A corporation can open bank accounts, hold property, and hire staff in its own name. And a properly drafted ONCA corporation, with charitable purposes and appropriate dissolution clauses, is a solid foundation for a later CRA charity application.

## Step-by-step: how to incorporate

1. **Search and clear your name with an Ontario-biased NUANS report.** Ontario requires an Ontario-biased (weighted) Nuans name search report for your proposed name, obtained from a private search provider — the ministry does not produce it for you. The report cannot be dated more than 90 days before you file your articles, and you will need the report reference number, the exact name searched, and the report date when you file.

2. **Prepare your articles of incorporation.** The articles set out the corporation's name, its purposes, the registered office address in Ontario, and the classes of members with their voting rights. ONCA requires at least one class of voting members. If you intend to apply for charitable status later, draft your purposes and dissolution provisions with CRA requirements in mind — rewording them afterwards means filing articles of amendment.

3. **Line up at least three directors.** ONCA requires a minimum of three directors. Each must be an individual at least 18 years old, not an undischarged bankrupt, and not found incapable of managing property. Terms can run up to four years; if the by-laws are silent, a director's term defaults to one year.

4. **Confirm your registered office.** Your corporation needs an Ontario registered office address where official documents can be delivered. This address goes into the articles and must be kept current through the registry.

5. **File through the Ontario Business Registry.** File online through the [Ontario Business Registry](https://www.ontario.ca/page/ontario-business-registry-all-services) with a credit or debit card, or by mail to the ministry's Toronto filing address with a cheque payable to the Minister of Finance. The government fee is **$155** either way; online filings are processed in about **5 business days**, mail filings in about 15. There is no expedited service for not-for-profit incorporations.

6. **Receive your incorporation package.** Once approved, you receive a certificate of incorporation, the official copy of your articles, a payment receipt, and a **company key** — keep the company key safe, because it is your credential for all future online filings. Then hold your first board meeting, adopt by-laws, and admit members. If you do not pass an organizational by-law within 60 days, ONCA's standard organizational by-law applies automatically until you adopt your own.

## After incorporation: ongoing compliance

Ontario not-for-profits file an **annual return** through the Ontario Business Registry to keep their corporate information current — there is no government fee, and online filings are processed immediately. Keep your company key handy, since it is required to transact online. You must also file updates when things change: a new registered office address, changes of directors, or amendments to your articles.

ONCA requires an **annual meeting of members**, at which financial statements — and any audit or review engagement report — are presented. The level of financial review depends on revenues and corporation type: a public benefit corporation with annual revenue under $100,000 can waive both audit and review if members agree; between $100,000 and $500,000 it must at least conduct a review engagement; at $500,000 or more an audit is mandatory. Other not-for-profits have more flexibility below $500,000.

Neglect has real consequences: corporations that fail to keep filings current risk cancellation, and stale records are a common reason grant applications stall. A maintained minute book — articles, by-laws, registers, minutes — is the paper trail funders and banks expect.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many board members do I need for a nonprofit in Ontario?

Ontario's Not-for-Profit Corporations Act, 2010 requires every not-for-profit corporation to have at least three directors. Each director must be an individual at least 18 years old, not an undischarged bankrupt, and not found incapable of managing property. Director terms can run up to four years, defaulting to one year if the by-laws are silent.

### How much does it cost to incorporate a not-for-profit in Ontario?

The Ontario government filing fee for articles of incorporation under ONCA is $155, whether you file online through the Ontario Business Registry or by mail. Add the cost of an Ontario-biased NUANS name search report from a private provider. The annual return afterwards is free to file online.

### Do I need a NUANS report to incorporate a nonprofit in Ontario?

Yes. Ontario requires an Ontario-biased (weighted) NUANS name search report for your proposed corporate name, purchased from a private search company. The report cannot be dated more than 90 days before you file your articles, and you must supply its reference number, the exact name searched, and the report date.

### What is the ONCA transition deadline, and does it still matter?

ONCA came into force on October 19, 2021 and gave existing Ontario not-for-profits three years — until October 18, 2024 — to update letters patent and by-laws. That deadline has passed, so older corporations should align their documents with ONCA now. New corporations are incorporated directly under ONCA and need no transition.

### Is an Ontario not-for-profit the same as a registered charity?

No. Incorporating under ONCA creates a not-for-profit corporation, but only the Canada Revenue Agency can grant registered charity status, which is what allows an organization to issue donation tax receipts. Many organizations incorporate in Ontario first, then apply to the CRA — drafting charity-ready purposes in the articles makes that much easier.

### Does an Ontario not-for-profit need an audit?

It depends on revenue and corporation type. A public benefit corporation must have an audit once annual revenue reaches $500,000; between $100,000 and $500,000 it needs at least a review engagement; below $100,000 members can waive both. Other not-for-profits can waive audit and review below $500,000 with member approval.
